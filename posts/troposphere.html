troposphere: plumbing for the atmosphere

co-written with claude because I am tired and undercaffeinated

I spent a Saturday building a piece of trust and safety infrastructure, and I want to explain what it is and why it matters without burying you in acronyms.

Most people have heard of Bluesky by now. Fewer people know that underneath it there is an open protocol called the AT Protocol, which a lot of us have taken to calling the atmosphere. The premise is that the social web should not be owned by any single company, and that you ought to be able to run your own server and build your own app while still reaching everyone else on the network. It is the closest thing I have seen in a while to the more pluralist internet I keep writing about, the one where more than one company has a say in how our shared spaces are run.

An open network comes with an open question, which is who keeps it safe. On a typical platform a single company handles moderation out of sight, but when the network is spread across many different servers and apps, moderation has to be able to travel between them. The way the atmosphere does this is through labelers, which are small services that look at a post or an account and attach a label to it, something like spam or graphic media. Apps subscribe to whichever labelers they trust, and each app gets to decide for itself whether a given label means hide this, or blur this, or add a warning, or do nothing at all. Nobody is forced to accept anyone else's judgment about what is harmful, which I find to be one of the more hopeful things about the way it was designed.

Bluesky built a tool called Ozone for running a labeler, and it works well, but it is their stack and it is shaped around the way they operate. At ROOST, where I work, we build something called Coop, which is an open-source console where human reviewers sit and work through a queue of flagged content, decide what breaks policy, and act on it. The problem I kept running into is that if you were building your own project on the atmosphere, with your own kinds of posts and your own server and your own app for viewing them, there was no clean way to connect that project to Coop and moderate it the way the rest of the network expects. Coop and the atmosphere did not really know how to talk to each other.

What I built over the weekend is the thing that lets them talk. I named it troposphere, after the lowest layer of the atmosphere, the one where the weather happens and where birds actually fly, and it sits in between the open network and Coop and carries information back and forth between them. I have been describing it to people as plumbing, because that is more or less what it is, a set of pipes that move the right things to the right places.

It does a handful of things. It pulls content off the network and into Coop so that reviewers have something to look at. It publishes labels that are signed with your own cryptographic key, which is what lets everyone else on the network trust that a label really came from you and not from someone pretending to be you. It takes in reports from people who flag something and puts those reports into Coop's review queue alongside everything else. And when a reviewer decides that a piece of content needs to come down rather than just be labeled, it can carry out that takedown on your own server.

The thing that matters most to me is that none of this assumes you are Bluesky. You can have your own kinds of content, your own server, and your own app for reading it, and troposphere will still do its job, because it does the atproto-specific work so that Coop does not have to. Coop stays general and never has to learn anything about the atmosphere in particular. That distinction sounds small, but it is most of the reason open infrastructure is worth building, because it means that a tiny community, or a co-op, or a project run out of another country can stand up real safety tooling without having to contort themselves to fit one large company's assumptions.

I should be upfront about how this got made. I built most of it in a single day working next to Claude Code, which is an AI coding agent. I made the design decisions and the judgment calls and read what it wrote, and it did a great deal of the typing and the testing while I directed it. I pointed it at the live Bluesky firehose and watched real labels get signed and checked against a real key, watched reports get routed, watched content come down on a test server. I am telling you this because I think we owe people some honesty about how software comes together now, and because I would rather say it plainly than have someone find it out later. The documentation says outright that an AI generated it, and I am the person you write to when something is wrong.

It is open source under the Apache 2.0 license. The code is on GitHub, and it is also on Tangled, which is git hosting built on top of the atmosphere itself, and putting it there felt right to me, keeping the source in the same place as the network it serves.

I am not going to pretend that a weekend project fixes anything about the internet. What it does is add one more thing to the toolbox, one more piece of infrastructure that a person can pick up and run on behalf of the people they are trying to look after. I want the work of keeping a community safe to belong to that community instead of being rented back to it by whoever happens to own the servers, and this is a small step in that direction. That is the internet I have spent most of my life caring about, and I am not done trying to protect it.
